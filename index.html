In token-level RL, the action space is the model's vocabulary.

Timing: 2 minutes. Let students vote before revealing B. A tokenizer maps text to token IDs before the model processes it; the model outputs a probability distribution over the vocabulary; sampling selects the next token ID; the detokenizer maps the growing token sequence back to readable text. For the simplest token-level RL view, one action is one token ID. Later slides broaden “action” to a reasoning span, tool call, or full trajectory when the learning setup groups several token choices together. Final-review cue: state = prompt plus generated context; action = next sampled token; policy = next-token distribution; detokenizer = representation conversion, not the policy or reward.

PRE-ASSESSMENT

02

A sampled action has positive advantage. What should policy optimization do?

AIncrease that action's probability in a similar state
BDecrease every action's probability
CReplace the policy with the reward model
DKeep the policy distribution unchanged
ANSWER · A

Positive advantage reinforces an action; negative advantage suppresses it.

Timing: 2 minutes. Reveal A, then ask what should happen when advantage is negative: the sampled action should become less likely in similar states. “Advantage” means performance relative to a baseline, not raw reward alone. Policy-gradient methods increase the log-probability of actions weighted by positive advantage and decrease it when advantage is negative. This qualitative rule is the bridge to GRPO; no gradient derivation is required. Final-review cue: positive advantage → increase log-probability; negative advantage → decrease it; near-zero advantage → little or no directional learning signal.

Shao et al. · DeepSeekMath · 2024

Timing: 1.5 minutes. GRPO begins on-policy: for each prompt q, sample G complete outputs from the frozen “old” policy used for this batch. They are not G labels and they are not retrieved demonstrations; they are alternative trajectories produced by the model itself. A reward function then scores every trajectory. This example uses a binary exact-answer verifier, but a reward can also combine correctness, format, safety, or a learned preference score. Teaching check: ask why all four outputs must answer the same prompt. The answer is that the group is a local comparison set: difficulty is held roughly constant while solution quality varies. Final-review cue: prompt q → sample {o₁,…,oG} from πold → compute one reward ri for each output. [Sources] Shao et al., DeepSeekMath. https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300

GRPO · STEP 2

Convert rewards into relative advantage.

GROUP-NORMALIZED ADVANTAGE

Worse than this group's average → negative

o₂−0.58
o₃−0.58
o₄−0.58

Timing: 1.5 minutes. “Advantage” means better or worse than a baseline. Here the rewards are [1,0,0,0], so the group mean is 0.25 and the population standard deviation is about 0.433. Normalization gives approximately [+1.73, −0.58, −0.58, −0.58]. The advantages sum to approximately zero: GRPO is learning which sampled output was better relative to its peers on this prompt. PPO typically estimates a baseline with a learned critic/value model. GRPO avoids that separate critic and uses group statistics instead, reducing memory use. The small ε in the denominator prevents numerical failure when reward variance is almost zero. If every reward is identical, every relative advantage is approximately zero and this group supplies no directional policy signal. Final-review cue: Ai = (ri − group mean) / (group standard deviation + ε). Positive means reinforce; negative means suppress; all-equal rewards mean no comparison. [Sources] Shao et al., DeepSeekMath. https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300

GRPO · STEP 3

With outcome supervision, every token inherits the trajectory's advantage.

SUCCESSFUL OUTPUT · o₁
12x=36;x=3

one final rewardr₁ = 1

OUTCOME SUPERVISION

The same normalized trajectory reward weights each sampled token.

Useful simplificationNo token-level labels are required.Real limitationThe signal cannot identify which exact reasoning step caused success or failure.

Shao et al. · DeepSeekMath · §4.1.2

Timing: 1.5 minutes. Outcome-supervised GRPO receives one reward at the end of a completion. After group normalization, it assigns that same advantage to every token in the completion. The optimizer still works token by token because the model factorizes a completion into conditional next-token probabilities. A successful trajectory therefore reinforces all sampled token choices along that trajectory; a worse-than-average trajectory suppresses them. Emphasize the limitation: the final reward does not reveal whether “12x = 36,” the semicolon, or “x = 3” was the decisive step. This is temporal credit assignment. Process supervision can provide rewards at intermediate reasoning steps, but it requires a trustworthy process evaluator. Final-review cue: outcome supervision gives one normalized trajectory reward; GRPO broadcasts it across the trajectory's tokens; token-level likelihoods are still what the optimizer changes. [Sources] Shao et al., DeepSeekMath, Section 4.1.2. https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300

GRPO · STEP 4

Update token probabilities — but do not let one batch move the policy too far.

01 · RATIO

Measure how much the new policy changed a sampled token.

02 · CLIPPING

Stop an extreme ratio from dominating the update.

03 · KL PENALTY

Discourage drifting too far from a reference policy.

Direction comes from advantage. Clipping and KL regularization control the size of the move.

Timing: 1.5 minutes. Read the objective from right to left rather than deriving it. The KL term discourages the current policy from drifting too far from a reference model. The clipped minimum limits how much a large probability ratio can improve the objective. The advantage supplies direction: positive advantage favors the sampled token; negative advantage disfavors it. Define the ratio carefully: ρi,t compares the current policy's probability of a sampled token with the old policy probability that generated the batch. If ρ = 1, the probability is unchanged; ρ > 1 means it increased; ρ < 1 means it decreased. Clipping does not freeze learning—it limits the incentive for excessively large changes on one batch. The reference policy in the KL term and the old sampling policy have different roles. Final-review cue: old policy generates data; current policy is optimized; reference policy anchors behavior. These can coincide at initialization but are conceptually distinct. [Sources] Shao et al., DeepSeekMath. https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300

Timing: 1.5 minutes. Use this as the cumulative retrieval slide. Hide the earlier details and ask students to reconstruct the loop from the five verbs: sample, score, normalize, optimize, repeat. Then ask what GRPO removes relative to PPO: a separately trained critic/value model. It does not remove the reward function, the policy, the old sampling policy, or the need for stable updates. Common exam confusions: 1. GRPO is on-policy in the practical sense that it repeatedly samples fresh outputs from a recent policy; it is not ordinary supervised learning on a fixed answer dataset. 2. “Group-relative” refers to the baseline used for advantage estimation, not to multiple models voting on one answer. 3. A verifier supplies scores, not corrected target sequences. 4. Equal rewards produce near-zero normalized advantages, so diversity without reward variation is not enough. 5. Removing the critic reduces memory, but sampling G completions and running verifiers still costs substantial compute. Final-review cue: be able to narrate one iteration and explain why the group replaces the critic baseline. [Sources] Shao et al., DeepSeekMath, Algorithm 1 and Section 4.1. https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300
